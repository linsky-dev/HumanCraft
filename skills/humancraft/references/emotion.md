# Emotion: first impression, use, lasting meaning

Use the three lenses together, not as rigid brain categories: immediate sensory response (visceral), task experience (behavioral), and interpretation/memory/identity (reflective). Aesthetics can influence perceived experience but cannot prove usability or excuse inaccessible behavior.

- Immediate: establish a visual language suited to context, readable hierarchy, typography, spacing, coherent colors, and calm first impression. Avoid generic decoration where it obscures information.
- During use: minimize preventable frustration, maintain fast and intelligible feedback, make success and recovery clear, preserve control. Sound and motion must be purposeful, optional where possible, and respect reduced-motion needs.
- Afterwards: leave a truthful completion receipt, meaningful accomplishment, clear next steps and trustworthy history. Support customization when it benefits users without creating unnecessary setup.

Design for the emotion a task warrants: reassurance in financial or account recovery, focus in enterprise operations, play in a game. Avoid forced celebration, guilt, anxiety, fake urgency, deceptive consent and addictive reward loops. Do not claim to know a person's emotion without evidence.

Verify visual hierarchy, language and feedback with a realistic task; ask users to describe confidence, trust and perceived effort. Capture what users can actually do as well as what they like. Treat the three lenses as a conceptual design framework, not diagnostic neuroscience.

## Advanced behavioral checks
Trust is earned by repeated reliability; calm verified recovery is better than cheerful false success. Compare expectations, actual outcomes and receipts; assign independent review. See HC-B08/B09/B12. [Contracts](behavior-contracts.md).

## Visual craft in actual software
- Derive a visual hierarchy from the user's next decision: make the primary task obvious, keep supporting actions nearby, and separate destructive actions without hiding them. Avoid styling every surface as a card.
- Use established design tokens where present. Choose readable type, line lengths, alignment and spacing that support scanning real content, not a decorative screenshot. Match density to task frequency; a professional data grid may need compact rows and a consumer onboarding flow more breathing room.
- Design realistic long labels, localized text, empty results, validation, loading and long lists before polishing the ideal state. Ensure contrast, visible focus, non-color cues, zoom and reflow.
- Use icons only when recognizable or paired with labels; visual affordance must match actual behavior. Do not replace familiar controls with novel gestures for novelty.
- Before declaring visual improvement, compare the same real content and viewport; ask a reviewer unfamiliar with the implementation to identify the primary task, state, and next step. If only self-review is available, label it as such. Avoid invented aesthetic scores.
